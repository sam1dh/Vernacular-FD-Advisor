## How compounding works

Compounding means interest is calculated not only on your principal but also on interest already added. If an FD compounds quarterly, the bank computes interest every 3 months and adds it to the balance, so the next quarter's interest is earned on a slightly bigger amount. More frequent compounding gives a slightly higher final amount at the same headline rate.

## The maturity formula

For a cumulative FD: Maturity = P × (1 + r/n)^(n×t), where P is principal, r is the annual rate as a decimal, n is the number of compounding periods per year (4 for quarterly), and t is tenor in years. Example: ₹1,00,000 at 8.50% p.a. compounded quarterly for 1 year gives ₹1,00,000 × (1.085/4)^4 ≈ ₹1,08,839. A simple non-compounded calculation would give only ₹1,08,500, so quarterly compounding added about ₹339 extra.

## Quarterly compounding is the common default

Most Indian banks, including Small Finance Banks, compound FD interest quarterly. The effective yield is therefore a little above the headline rate: 8.50% p.a. compounded quarterly is about 8.77% effective annual yield. Advertisements sometimes show this higher effective yield, so compare the quoted rate, not just the yield.

## Maturity with senior citizen bonus

If a bank gives a 0.50% additional rate to senior citizens, the same formula applies with the higher rate. Example: ₹1,00,000 at 9.00% p.a. quarterly for 1 year ≈ ₹1,09,308.

## Part-year interest

When an FD is held for a period that is not a whole number of quarters, banks compute interest for the broken period at the applicable rate for the actual days held, usually on a simple (non-compounded) basis for the broken part.
